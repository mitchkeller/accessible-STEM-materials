Use of Generative AI.

The purpose of this course is to develop your ability to understand solve problems in combinatorics, including writing a couple of types of short proofs. The use of artificial intelligence (AI) tools and applications (ChatGPT, Copilot, DALL-E, or any others) does not support the learning objectives of this course and is prohibited. Using them in any way for written homework in this course is a violation of the course’s expectations and will be addressed through UW–Madison’s academic misconduct policy, specifically UWS 14.03(1)b (b) Uses unauthorized materials or fabricated data in any academic exercise.
🔗
Since this is a MATH course, I’ll say a bit more about what’s going on with the Large Language Models (LLMs) that underlie ‘generative AI’. LLMs are, largely, probabilistic autocorrect with a tendency to plagiarize when not making up nonsense. They generate text using linear algebra and probability. They have no idea if what they have generated is correct, because they are models and not intelligent beings. They often produce nonsense, which means that they can be useful tools for experts who can detect when the LLM is lying. For students who are trying to learn, however, you generally have not yet acquired the tools to be able to tell when the LLM has produced nonsense. Thus, using LLMs is counterproductive to your learning of mathematics!
🔗
Ethically, there are also many issues with LLMs. They consume a lot of energy and water, although the evidence on exactly how much is consumed on a per-query basis is still low quality and often speculative. Most of the text the LLMs have been trained on may have been used in violation of copyright or licensing terms. For instance, Applied Combinatorics has undoubtedly been ingested by LLMs as part of their training data. However, I don’t see any LLM complying with the CC-BY-SA license terms that require them to acknowledge the use of text from Applied Combinatorics and to share under the same license. Copyrighted works that do not have an open-source license have also been used as part of the training data. (Full disclosure: As a photographer, I do use Adobe’s generative remove tools to remove distractions from my photographs. Adobe has only used the Adobe Stock library as training data for their Firefly model and has compensated those who have contributed images to Adobe Stock for this use. Still doesn’t solve the energy and water usage piece of things, I will admit, but it does let me sleep at night in terms of use of intellectual property. I also do not use Adobe’s generative tools to create new images or to add things that were not originally in the image.)
