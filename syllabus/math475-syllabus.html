    Section Course Information

    Course Description
    🔗
    Problems of enumeration, distribution, and arrangement. Inclusion-exclusion principle. Generating functions and linear recurrence relations. Combinatorial identities. Graph coloring problems. Finite designs. Systems of distinct representatives and matching problems in graphs. Potential applications in the social, biological, and physical sciences. Puzzles. Problem solving.

    Learning Outcomes.

    1. Understand basic counting strategies, such as staged thought-experiments, inclusion/exclusion, generating functions, and recurrence relations, and apply these strategies to solve a wide variety of counting problems.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Recall basic objects that are used in combinatorics, such as permutations and combinations of sets and multisets, binomial and multinomial coefficients, the Catalan numbers, the Stirling numbers, and the partition numbers.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Analyze a given combinatorial problem using the standard theorems of combinatorics, such as the pigeonhole principle, the Newton binomial theorem, the multinomial theorem, the Ramsey theorem, the Dilworth theorem, the Burnside theorem, and the Polya counting theorem.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Construct mathematical arguments related to combinatorial problems using the above definitions, properties, theorems, and counting strategies; including the construction of examples and counterexamples.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Convey his or her arguments in oral and written form in English, using appropriate mathematical terminology, notation, and grammar.
      🔗
      🔗

    Section Assessments

    Written Weekly Homework.

    Each week you will submit solutions to selected homework problems. Some of the problems will be graded for correctness by a grader assigned to work for me. Others will be graded for completness. Homework will be submitted via Gradescope and is due by 2300 (almost) every Tuesday, starting in the third week of classes. For computing course grades, each homework homework grade will be normalized to be a real number between \(0\) and \(4\) (possibly slightly higher than \(4\)) in a manner specified on Canvas. Your lowest homework score will be dropped, and the other \(10\) will be averaged.
    🔗
    For graded homework problems, tests, and the final exam, each problem will be assigned an integer score between \(0\) and \(4\) based on the following rubric:
    🔗
    Grade Description
    4
    Good work with minor (generally computational) errors. Explanation complete or has only small gaps. (\(\sim\)A)
    🔗
    3
    Good work with more serious (generally conceptual) errors. Explanation is complete, but may be insufficiently clear. (\(\sim\)B)
    🔗
    2
    Significant, but incomplete, work that could clearly lead to a correct answer is presented, and some attempt at explanation is made. (\(\sim\)C)
    🔗
    1
    Some work that might lead to a complete solution is presented. Work, even if correct, without explanation cannot receive a higher score. (\(\sim\)D)
    🔗
    0
    No progress, no relevant information, or illegible. (\(\sim\)F)
    🔗
    Occasionally, exceptional work will receive a grade of \(5\text{,}\) generally based on high-quality explanations and/or very organized computations accompanying the explanations. A score of \(5\) should be viewed as extra credit for outstanding work and not a routinely-expected score.
    🔗
    🔗

    Peer Instruction Participation.

    “Peer instruction” describes an instructional strategy that involves answering (typically) multiple choice questions using a technological solution, having you discuss your answers with your peers after voting has closed, and then voting again. Although this strategy developed in physics, its efficacy in a number of areas has been demonstrated through educational research. We will frequently use peer instruction in class, and Runestone Academy will be our means of voting. A component of your grade will be based on the fact that you have voted on these questions. Because they are part of the learning experience, correctness of responses will not be part of your grade. You will receive an integer score for peer instruction participation determined by the percentage of all peer instruction questions you answer over the course of the semester.
    🔗
    PI Grade/Prep Grade 5 4 3 2 1 0
    Percent Answered \([90,100]\) \([80,90)\) \([70,80)\) \([60,70)\) \([50,60)\) \([0,50)\)

    Class Preparation Assignments.

    In order to be able to effectively use our time together in class, before most class meetings you will have a class preparation assignment that will be submitted via Runestone Academy. A typical class preparation assignment will consist of reading assigned portions of the textbook and answering some questions to help me assess what parts of the reading need the most discussion in classtime. Because these assignments are part of the learning experience, correctness of responses will not be part of your grade. You will receive an integer score for your overall class preparation assignment grade. It will be determined by the percentage of all points you earn for class preparation assignments over the course of the semester. The same scale will be used as for peer instruction grades above.

    Weekly Exit Tickets.

    We will spend most of each class period actively working together through group activities to help you develop a deep understanding of the course content. In order to help me keep track of your learning progress throughout the semester, you’ll complete a brief exit ticket on Canvas each week. Exit Tickets will be due every Thursday by 2300. Each exit ticket will be worth four points, and your exit ticket grade will be a real number between \(0\) and \(4\) that is the average of your scores on your exit tickets with the lowest exit ticket dropped.

    Section Course-Specific Policies

    Use of Generative AI.

    The purpose of this course is to develop your ability to understand solve problems in combinatorics, including writing a couple of types of short proofs. The use of artificial intelligence (AI) tools and applications (ChatGPT, Copilot, DALL-E, or any others) does not support the learning objectives of this course and is prohibited. Using them in any way for written homework in this course is a violation of the course’s expectations and will be addressed through UW–Madison’s academic misconduct policy, specifically UWS 14.03(1)b (b) Uses unauthorized materials or fabricated data in any academic exercise.
    🔗
    Since this is a MATH course, I’ll say a bit more about what’s going on with the Large Language Models (LLMs) that underlie ‘generative AI’. LLMs are, largely, probabilistic autocorrect with a tendency to plagiarize when not making up nonsense. They generate text using linear algebra and probability. They have no idea if what they have generated is correct, because they are models and not intelligent beings. They often produce nonsense, which means that they can be useful tools for experts who can detect when the LLM is lying. For students who are trying to learn, however, you generally have not yet acquired the tools to be able to tell when the LLM has produced nonsense. Thus, using LLMs is counterproductive to your learning of mathematics!
    🔗
    Ethically, there are also many issues with LLMs. They consume a lot of energy and water, although the evidence on exactly how much is consumed on a per-query basis is still low quality and often speculative. Most of the text the LLMs have been trained on may have been used in violation of copyright or licensing terms. For instance, Applied Combinatorics has undoubtedly been ingested by LLMs as part of their training data. However, I don’t see any LLM complying with the CC-BY-SA license terms that require them to acknowledge the use of text from Applied Combinatorics and to share under the same license. Copyrighted works that do not have an open-source license have also been used as part of the training data. (Full disclosure: As a photographer, I do use Adobe’s generative remove tools to remove distractions from my photographs. Adobe has only used the Adobe Stock library as training data for their Firefly model and has compensated those who have contributed images to Adobe Stock for this use. Still doesn’t solve the energy and water usage piece of things, I will admit, but it does let me sleep at night in terms of use of intellectual property. I also do not use Adobe’s generative tools to create new images or to add things that were not originally in the image.)
    🔗
    🔗

    Citing Resources.

    With each week’s homework, you must turn in a one-paragraph description of all the resources you used on that homework. This includes anyone (student, professor, roommate, anyone) that you talked with about the problems, as well as any book or online resource (website, PDF, social media post, email) that helped in your solution. Writing such a paragraph is a required part of each week’s homework. Even if you work on the problems entirely on your own and your only resource is the book, you are required to write this paragraph. Moreover, while it will be acceptable to use any resources other than Generative AI that you would like in writing up your homework solutions, failure to honestly cite the resources that you use will be considered an instance of academic dishonesty. Here is a sample paragraph:
    🔗
    “On this week’s homework, I worked collaboratively with Alice Smith, Bob Jones, and Carol Thompson on basically all of the problems. We used a technique that we learned from a You Tube video (http://youtube.blah.com) to more simply factor Gaussian integers. We checked our answers against an online solution key we found at this webpage: http://blah.blah.edu. We also got really stuck on Problem 5, and so we used the solution in that answer key as the basis for Problem 5.”
    🔗

    Deadlines and Extensions.

    Here is how deadlines work in the real world: They exist, and they’re important. However, there’s a certain amount of flexibility with them. If you need a little longer on something, you email whoever has set you the deadline and ask if you can have a few more days. This is usually not a big deal, but if it happens a lot, people will start asking you if everything is all right. That is also how deadlines for homework and exit tickets work in this class. Submit the form linked on Canvas to ask for a deadline change on anything you need, and that’s mostly fine. If you ask for lots of changes, we’ll work together to find ways to help you keep up with the work in the course. Because of the critical nature of the timing of peer instruction responses and class preparation assignments, due date extensions do not apply to these.
    🔗

    Names and Pronouns.

    Professional courtesy and sensitivity are especially important with respect to individuals and topics dealing with differences of race, culture, religion, politics, sexual orientation, gender, gender variance, and nationalities. UW-Madison has options available to set your name in use, which is reflected in most systems where I will see your name. I will gladly address you by an alternate name and the correct gender pronoun. Please advise me of this information early in the semester so that I may make appropriate changes to my records.
    🔗
    My pronouns are he/him/his. I ask that you address me as “Prof. Keller” or “Dr. Keller”, the appropriate professional forms. Since this is not high school, please do not address me as “Mr. Keller”. In general, you will find that I am not an overly formal individual. However, academia has a long history of marginalizing scholars—professors, librarians, and students—who don’t present as the Hollywood image of a professor (older, white, male). Using titles for all your instructors is a small way that we can make the academy more inclusive. Therefore, in solidarity with my colleagues of all identities and backgrounds, “Prof. Keller” is the form of address that I prefer.
    🔗
    🔗

    Learning differences and special needs.

    There are a variety of ways in which a person can be successful academically, and it often takes time for each of us to learn what is required for us to succeed. Some learners have different abilities that warrant special attention in order to create an optimal learning environment. One aspect of this course that could make some learners uncomfortable is frequent in-class groupwork and occasional presentations to the class. Some people thrive when working together and like to jump right in, while others prefer to have time to reflect before discussing their ideas. This diversity of approach makes us all stronger. When working in groups, please be respectful of your classmates’ approaches, which may involve allowing a group member some time to work quietly before joining a discussion.
    🔗
    Please come speak with me about any concerns you have about creating a suitable learning environment for you in this course. For some accommodations, the University requires professional documentation of a disability before that accommodation can be approved. (See “Accommodations for Students with Disabilities” on the policies page of the Guide.) However, there are small things I can do in terms of group assigments and classroom culture that can create a better place to learn for all of us, and I am happy to discuss your needs with you.
    🔗
    🔗

    Incomplete Grades.

    Incomplete grades will be assigned only under extraordinary circumstances in accordance with L&S policy, which states as follows:
    🔗
    “An Incomplete (I) may be reported for a student who has been enrolled in a course with a passing grade until near the end of the semester/term and then, due to illness or some other unusual and substantiated cause beyond the student’s control, has been unable to take or complete the final examination (or to complete some limited amount of term work). An Incomplete is not given to a student who stays away from a final examination unless the student proves to the instructor that he or she was prevented from attending as indicated above. In the absence of such proof the grade reported will be an F. Even when a student can provide verifiable documentation, a student may still earn a grade of F if his/her work convinces the instructor that the student cannot successfully pass the course.”
    🔗
    🔗
    If you feel an incomplete grade may be warranted, you must contact me before the end of the final examination period on Thursday, 18 December 2025.
